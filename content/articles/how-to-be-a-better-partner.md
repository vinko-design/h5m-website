To be a better partner, start by asking the person you're with what "better" would mean to them, and tell them what it would mean to you. Each of you names one way you'd like to do better for the other and one thing you'd love from them. Then you choose one of those to look after together for the next week or two. You notice when it happens, and you adjust when a week doesn't go as planned.

You may have been thinking you could be doing more for your partner, or they may have said something that left you wondering what they'd like. Either one is a good reason to start. You don't need a long list of improvements. One thing you've both chosen gives you something you can actually do this week, and you'll both know when it's going well.

## What does being a better partner mean for the two of you?

It means doing more of what helps your partner feel loved and supported, and those things differ from one person to the next. One person feels cared for when their partner remembers to ask how the dentist appointment went. Another hardly notices that, and is quietly delighted when their partner does the dishes on an evening they're worn out. You could put a lot of effort into asking about appointments and still miss what your partner was hoping for.

Asking puts their answer in place of your guess. You don't have to read their mind or copy what someone else's partner does. You find out what would mean the most to them right now, which may have changed since a new job, a move, or a busy few months.

It also goes both ways. Wanting to be better is a generous thing to bring to a relationship, and your partner can bring the same to you. When both of you say what you'd like, "better" becomes something you've decided on together, instead of a list one person wrote alone.

## Which everyday things tend to make a difference?

A few ordinary habits come up again and again when people talk about good partners. Any of them could be where the two of you begin.

Listening is the first. Say your partner comes home and starts telling you about a meeting that went badly. It's natural to answer with a story about your own boss, but your partner may hear that as you taking over the conversation. Try staying with their story a little longer. Ask what happened next, or how they feel about it now. Your own story can wait until they've finished.

Small kindness and appreciation are another. That might mean making their coffee the way they like it, sending a message when you're running late, or thanking them for something specific, like sorting out the car insurance you'd both been putting off. In a [five-week study that followed couples day by day](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9271060/), giving one partner a short plan for expressing gratitude led them to express more of it, and those couples spent more of their everyday time in each other's company than couples in the comparison group. The study measured time together, and it's one group of couples, so take it as a reason to say thanks more often rather than a promise about everything else. It's also something one person can begin alone, without asking anything of the other.

Time together is a third. When the evenings fill up with errands and work email, you can share a home for weeks and barely talk. Ten minutes with the phones away, a walk after dinner, or cooking together can give you a chance to pay attention to each other. If finding any shared evening at all is what's missing, [making time for your partner](/blog/how-to-make-time-for-your-partner) covers how to find a window in a full week.

Looking after your own health and life matters too, even though it can sound like the opposite of focusing on your partner. If you've slept badly all week, you may have less patience for a long story at dinner. Friends, exercise, and something you enjoy on your own give you energy and things to share. The same goes for your partner. Encouraging their running, their evening class, or their night out with friends is part of being a good partner as well.

Treat these as ideas to choose from. Your partner's answer tells you which one matters to them now, and it might be something else entirely.

## How can you start if your partner hasn't asked for this?

Start with yourself and what you'd like to do, so it sounds like an offer. Pick a moment when neither of you is rushing, like a slow weekend breakfast or a walk, and ask whether now works.

You could say, "I've been thinking about how I could be a better partner to you. Could we each say what would mean the most to us right now? We could do it now, or another evening this week if that suits you better."

Your partner might be curious and want to start right away. They might ask whether something's wrong. Tell them honestly what made you think of it, whether that's a small worry or just wanting more good evenings together. They might suggest the weekend, or say they'd rather not right now. Each of those is a fair answer. If it's a no for now, you can mention it again another week, and you don't need to talk them into it.

If they say everything's already great, that's lovely to hear. You can still ask what they'd love more of. Wanting to start doesn't make you the one with a problem, and it doesn't mean your partner has one.

## How does the exercise work?

You'll each need a piece of paper or a note on your phone, and half an hour or so when you won't be interrupted.

![Two partners sit at a kitchen table, each writing a few lines on their own sheet of paper.](/blog/how-to-be-a-better-partner/kitchen-table.webp)

1. On your own, write one way you'd like to be a better partner right now.
2. Write one thing you'd love more of from the other person.
3. Next to each, write what it would look like on an ordinary day.
4. Take turns reading one line aloud, and say back what you heard.
5. Look at all four lines together and choose one to look after.

Writing on your own first means each of you puts down what's true for you before hearing the other's answers. Keep each line specific. "Be more present" is a good feeling to start from, and "put my phone in the other room when we eat dinner" is something your partner can actually see you do. In the same way, "I'd love more help" becomes clearer as "I'd love it if you took the trash out on Thursdays." If you go blank when you try to write what you'd love from your partner, the guide on [naming your needs in a relationship](/blog/needs-in-a-relationship) has gentle questions to help you find the words.

When you read, swap turns after each line, so one of you isn't reading everything while the other waits. The listener's job is to understand. Say back what you heard in your own words, like "So you'd love it if I asked about your day before we started on dinner plans?", and ask one question if something isn't clear. Explanations can wait. If something your partner would love surprises you, that's useful. It's something you didn't know an hour ago.

Then lay the four lines side by side. Often two of them point at the same thing. Say you wrote "listen properly when you talk about work" and your partner wrote "I'd love you to ask how my day went." Those fit together, and they make a natural place to start. If nothing overlaps, choose a line that matters to at least one of you and that you could act on this week. It might be your own change, your partner's, or something you'll both do.

Whichever you pick, you'll both look after it. One of you might do most of the doing, like asking about the other's day each evening. The other looks after it by noticing, by saying thanks when it happens, and by mentioning it if the plan stops fitting the week. That's what makes it something you share.

Finish by saying who does what, and when, and agree on a day to look back at how it's going. "I'll ask about your day before we look at our phones on weeknights, and we'll talk about it again in two weeks" is clear enough for both of you. The other three lines stay on the paper for later. You don't need to drop them, and you don't need to do all of them at once.

## How do you keep it a conversation, without grading each other?

Both of you answer, both of you listen, and neither of you hands out marks. The lines on your paper describe what each of you would like next, and nobody is deciding who has been a good or bad partner.

Either of you can skip a line or stop the conversation at any point, without giving a reason. If your partner says, "Can we leave the second one for another day?", take that as the answer and stop there. Being free to say no makes it easier for both of you to say yes.

You don't have to pretend you haven't noticed anything. If something your partner does has been on your mind, whether it's a lovely habit you'd like more of or something that's been bothering you, you can write it down as your own experience and bring it. Say it as what you felt: "I loved it when you called me at lunch last week," or "I've felt a bit on my own in the evenings lately." What you leave out is a tally of every time something did or didn't happen.

What your partner tells you is theirs. If one of their answers turns up later in an argument, they may say less next time. Keeping it safe means they'll feel free to tell you more.

Talking like this is one way to look after your relationship. It's worth doing on an ordinary week when everything feels fine, because it keeps you both up to date on what the other would love. And when something does feel a little off, you already have a calm way to talk about it.

## What happens over the next few weeks?

Over the next week or two, you both pay attention to the one thing you chose. Noticing is as simple as saying something when you see it happen: "Thanks for asking about my day, I liked that." A small comment like that tells the other person their effort was seen, and it's nice to hear.

Some weeks won't go to plan. Someone gets ill, a deadline takes up the evenings, or the habit just slips. When that happens, say so without blame, and look at whether the plan still fits. Maybe weeknights are too packed and Sundays would work better. Moving the plan somewhere it fits counts as progress, and so does trying again the next week.

On the day you agreed, sit down and ask each other two things: did it happen, and did it help? If it did, keep it going. If it didn't fit, change it, or choose another line from your papers. What "better" means can shift over time, so it's worth asking each other again every so often. If you'd like a regular conversation that looks at more of your life together, these [relationship check-in questions](/blog/relationship-check-in) give it a simple shape, at whatever interval suits you.

When the one thing becomes part of your weeks, say so. Maybe you've eaten dinner without phones every weeknight for two weeks, and you've both noticed you're talking more. That's a high-five moment. Celebrate it however suits the two of you, with an actual high five, a favorite meal, or a walk, and then pick the next thing if you'd like one.
