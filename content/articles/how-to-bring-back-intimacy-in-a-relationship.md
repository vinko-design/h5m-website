If a kind of intimacy you valued feels completely lost, start with a calm talk. Each of you says what kind of closeness you miss, and the other listens. Then choose one small next step you're both comfortable with, try it, and talk again later about how it went. Intimacy can mean time together, touch, feeling heard, sexual connection, or something else, and you may each miss a different kind.

You don't need a big plan or perfect words. If you've been wondering how to get the intimacy back in a relationship that still matters to you, a few honest sentences and some patience with each other are enough to begin.

## What can intimacy mean for each of you?

Intimacy is the closeness that makes you feel near your partner, and it can look quite different for each of you. For one person it's long talks after dinner and feeling that their partner really knows what's on their mind. For another it's a hand on the shoulder while cooking, or falling asleep close together. It can also be laughing at the same silly thing, making plans together, spending an unhurried Sunday side by side, or sexual connection.

This matters because it's easy to assume you both miss the same thing. If one of you misses talking and the other misses touch, a plan built around only one of those leaves the other person still missing something. Both kinds of closeness are real, and neither one is the right answer.

Sex can be part of the picture, and it's welcome in the conversation if it matters to either of you. It doesn't have to be the measure of whether closeness has come back. A lot of closeness can happen over a meal, on a walk, or in a quiet moment on the sofa.

## What can get in the way of closeness?

Changes in your daily life or in what you share with each other may be part of what you want to talk about. Perhaps your evenings look different now, or one of you has something on your mind that the other doesn't know about. Ask each other what you've noticed, without deciding in advance who or what is to blame.

The way you describe it shapes the whole talk. "Your late shifts mean we barely see each other" puts the problem on your partner. "Our evenings have disappeared since the late shifts started" describes something that's happening to both of you. The second version gives you both a shared situation to talk about.

## How can you bring it up gently?

Choose a calm, unhurried moment, and open with what you miss rather than what your partner isn't doing. A relaxed walk, a slow weekend morning, or a quiet evening at home is a kinder time than right after an argument, when one of you is about to leave, or in bed just after one of you has said no to sex.

It helps to ask first. Something like "There's something I've been thinking about, and it's nothing bad. Is now a good time, or would later tonight be better?" gives your partner a moment to get ready, and a real chance to say not now. If they'd rather wait, agree on a time and keep it.

When you start, talk about yourself and what you'd like more of: "I miss us. I keep thinking about the evenings we used to spend just talking, and I'd love more of that. How has it been for you?" That opening tells your partner what you want and asks for their side before you've said anything more. If you're the one who has noticed the distance, you can start on your own. Your partner doesn't need to have felt it first.

## How do you each say what you miss, and listen?

Take turns. One of you talks about the closeness you miss while the other listens to understand, and then you swap.

![One partner talks while the other listens closely on a sofa at home.](/blog/how-to-bring-back-intimacy-in-a-relationship/partners-talking-and-listening-on-sofa.webp)

The person talking stays with their own experience. "I feel close to you when we cook together" and "I miss you reaching for my hand" are easy to hear. "You never touch me anymore" is harder to hear, even when it comes from the same feeling.

The person listening has one job for now, which is to understand. Defending, explaining, and fixing can wait. Say back what you heard in your own words, such as "So you miss us just talking, without phones, and lately it feels like we only talk about chores." Then ask whether you got it right, and maybe one question to understand it better. A [review of research on how romantic partners respond to each other](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37515977/) describes feeling that your partner is responsive to you as basic to how intimacy develops and lasts. Listening gives each of you a chance to understand what the other misses before choosing a step.

Then swap. Your partner may miss something different, or may say things feel fine to them right now. That's useful to know too. Ask what makes them feel close to you these days, and listen to the answer with the same care.

## What small steps could you try first?

Start with something small enough that you could both say yes to it this week. Here are a few kinds of steps you can choose from:

1. More everyday touch, like holding hands, a long hug, or sitting close on the sofa
2. Saying one thing you appreciate about each other, out loud
3. Time set aside for just the two of you, with phones put away
4. Something new together, like a different walk, a new recipe, or a class
5. Sexual connection, if you both want it

A small step is easy to agree to, and easy to change if it doesn't feel right. Trying to regain intimacy in a relationship all at once can turn it into a test you're both worried about failing.

Touch that doesn't have to lead anywhere can be a gentle place to begin, especially if sex has become a tense subject. A hug can be just a hug, if you both want one. Appreciation works in a similar way. Noticing something kind your partner did and saying so takes a few seconds, and it shows them that you still see the good things.

Something new gives you a shared experience to talk about afterwards, and it doesn't need to be big or expensive. Sexual connection is on the list because it may be the closeness you're missing. It sits alongside the others, as one choice you can make together if you both want to.

## How do you agree on a step you're both comfortable with?

Look for a step that both of you can say a real yes to, and treat any no as a fair answer.

Each of you can name one or two steps you'd like to try, then look for overlap. If you both picked time together, you've found your step. If you want different things, look for a step that gives each of you some of what you miss, like a slow evening walk where you hold hands. Or take turns, trying one person's choice first and the other's next.

A step only works if you both want it. Either of you can say no to a step, and either of you can agree and later change your mind, without it being held against you. That's true for every option on the list, and especially for sex. Neither partner should feel pushed into saying yes.

Hearing no can sting. When it happens, it helps to treat it as an answer about that one step and to ask what would feel better instead. "Okay, what would feel good to you?" keeps the conversation going. Neither of you owes the other a particular kind of closeness. You're looking for something you'd both enjoy.

## Should you set a frequency to aim for?

You can, if you both want one, as long as it stays a rough aim you've chosen together and can change at any time.

A loose aim can remind you to make time for closeness. It might sound like "Let's try to have an evening out about once a month" or "Let's aim to walk together most weekends." Those are only examples. The right aim is whatever the two of you pick, and it can look different from anyone else's.

Keep it flexible. A busy month, a cold, or a stressful week at work can change what's realistic, and you can lower the aim or pause it without anyone feeling they've let the other down. Treat the number as a shared hope, and never as a score or something to count against your partner.

If you include sex in your aim, each time still depends on both of you wanting it then. The aim never outranks a no. If either of you starts to feel pressure from the number, drop it for a while and go back to talking about what you miss.

## How do you come back to it later?

Agree on when you'll talk again, then use that talk to keep what's working and change what isn't.

Choose an interval that suits you, such as a couple of weeks after you start, or once a month, and pick one you'll actually keep. When you sit down, ask each other what felt good, what didn't, and whether you'd like to keep the same step, change it, or add another. If closeness has slipped again, you can simply choose a new step together.

Notice the small wins, too. The first evening you spent talking for an hour, or the hug that ended with both of you laughing, deserves a mention. You can tell each other what you enjoyed.
