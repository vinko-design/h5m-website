When a type of intimacy you valued feels completely lost, one gentle way to begin is an honest conversation about it. Each of you says what kind of closeness you miss while the other listens. Together, you choose one small next step you're both comfortable with, try it for a while, and agree on a time to look back at how it's going. Intimacy can mean time together, touch, feeling heard, sexual connection, or something else, and each of you may miss a different type.

You don't need a big plan or perfect words. If you've been wondering how to get the intimacy back in a relationship that still matters to you, a few honest sentences and some patience with each other are enough to begin.

## What can intimacy mean for each of you?

Intimacy is the closeness that makes you feel near your partner, and the type that matters most may be quite different for each of you. For one person it's long talks after dinner and the feeling that their partner really knows what's on their mind. For another it's a hand on the shoulder while cooking, or falling asleep close together. It can also be laughing at the same silly thing, making plans together, or spending an unhurried Sunday side by side.

Knowing this helps, because it's easy to assume you both miss the same thing. Say one of you misses talking and the other misses touch. A plan built around only one of those leaves the other person still missing something. Both kinds of closeness are real, and neither one is the right answer.

Sexual connection can matter deeply to one or both of you, and it belongs in the conversation. You may find that you want different things, such as more or less sex, or a different kind of physical closeness. Those differences are worth talking about openly, and neither of you is wrong for wanting what you want. Sex also doesn't have to be the only measure of whether closeness has come back. A lot of it happens over a meal, on a walk, or in a quiet moment on the sofa.

## What can get in the way of closeness?

Changes in your daily life, or in what you share with each other, may be part of what you want to talk about. Perhaps your evenings look different now, or one of you has something on your mind that the other doesn't know about. Ask each other what you've noticed, without deciding in advance who or what is to blame.

The way you describe it shapes the whole conversation. "Your late shifts mean we barely see each other" puts the problem on your partner. "Our evenings have disappeared since the late shifts started" describes something that's happening to both of you. With the second version, you're looking at a shared situation together.

## How can you bring it up gently?

Choose a calm, unhurried moment, and open with what you miss rather than with what your partner isn't doing. A relaxed walk, a slow weekend morning, or a quiet evening at home is a kinder time than right after an argument, when one of you is about to leave, or in bed just after one of you has said no to sex.

Either of you can be the one to start. You don't have to wait until you both feel the distance, and bringing it up doesn't mean you're the one with the problem.

Asking first gives your partner a real choice about timing. You might say, "I'd love to talk with you about how close we've been feeling lately. It's nothing bad, I just miss some things about us. Would now work, or would you rather find an unhurried time later this week?" Hearing that, your partner may feel curious right away and want to talk now. That's fine if you both have the time. Some people would rather wait for a moment when nobody is rushed, and that's a fair preference too. Agree on a time and keep it.

When the conversation begins, talk about yourself and what you'd like more of: "I miss us. I keep thinking about the evenings we used to spend just talking, and I'd love more of that. How has it felt to you lately?" That last question asks your partner how close they've been feeling, so you hear their side before you say anything more.

## How do you each say what you miss, and listen?

Take turns. One of you talks about the closeness you miss while the other listens to understand, and then you swap.

Whoever is talking stays with their own experience. "I feel close to you when we cook together" and "I miss you reaching for my hand" are easy to hear. "You never touch me anymore" is harder to hear, even when it comes from the same feeling.

While you're listening, your only job is to understand. Defending, explaining, and fixing can wait. Say back what you heard in your own words, such as "So you miss us just talking, without phones, and lately it feels like we only talk about chores." Then ask whether you got it right, and maybe one question to understand it better. A [review of research on how romantic partners respond to each other](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37515977/) describes feeling that your partner is responsive to you as basic to how intimacy develops and lasts. Listening first gives each of you a chance to understand what the other misses before you choose a step.

When it's your partner's turn, they may miss something different, or they may say things feel fine to them right now. Both answers are useful to know. Ask what makes them feel close to you these days, and listen with the same care.

## What small steps could you try first?

Start with something small enough that you could both say yes to it this week. Here are a few kinds of steps to choose from:

- More everyday touch, like holding hands, a long hug, or sitting close on the sofa
- Saying one thing you appreciate about each other, out loud
- Time set aside for just the two of you, with phones put away
- Something new together, like a different walk, a new recipe, or a class
- Sexual connection, as something you choose together

A small step is easy to agree to, and easy to change if it doesn't feel right. Trying to regain intimacy in a relationship all at once can turn it into a test you're both worried about failing.

Touch that doesn't have to lead anywhere can be a gentle place to begin, especially when sex has become a tense subject. A hug can be just a hug. Appreciation works in a similar way. Noticing something kind your partner did and saying so takes a few seconds, and it shows them that you still see the good things.

Something new gives you a shared experience to talk about afterwards, and it doesn't need to be big or expensive. Sexual connection is on the list because it may be the closeness one or both of you miss most. It sits alongside the other options, as one choice the two of you make together.

## How do you agree on a step you're both comfortable with?

Look for a step you can both say a real yes to, and treat any no as a fair answer.

Each of you can name one or two steps you'd like to try, then look for overlap. When you've both picked time together, you've found your step. When you want different things, look for a step that gives each of you some of what you miss, like a slow evening walk where you hold hands. Or take turns, trying one person's choice first and the other's next.

A step only works when you both want it. Either of you can decline, and either of you can agree and later change your mind, without it being held against you. That's true for every option on the list, and especially for sex. Nobody should feel pushed into a yes.

Hearing no can sting. Accept it as an answer about that one step. A no doesn't need an explanation. Later, at a time that suits you both, you can gently ask whether anything would help that kind of closeness feel better for your partner, such as a slower pace, a different time of day, or a different kind of touch. Ask out of care, with no expectation that the answer changes, and let your partner decide whether they want to talk about it. Neither of you owes the other a particular kind of closeness. You're looking for something you'd both enjoy.

## Should you set a frequency to aim for?

You can, as long as it stays a rough aim you've chosen together and can change at any time.

A loose aim can remind you to make time for closeness. It might sound like "Let's try to have an evening out about once a month" or "Let's aim to walk together most weekends." Those are only examples. The right aim is whatever the two of you pick.

Keep it flexible. A packed month, a family visit, or a stressful stretch at work can change what feels realistic. Some weeks or months won't go the way you hoped, and that's part of ordinary life. When it happens, either of you can say so, check in with the other, adjust the aim or pause it, and try again.

Think of the aim as a shared hope you're working toward together. Reaching it is a high-five moment worth celebrating. It's never a score, and it's never something to count against your partner.

When sex is part of your aim, each time still depends on both of you wanting it at that particular moment. The aim never outranks a no. Either of you can speak up as soon as the aim starts to feel like pressure. You can set it aside for a while and go back to talking about what you miss.

## How do you come back to it later?

Agree on when you'll look back together, then use that conversation to keep what's working and change what isn't.

Choose an interval that suits you, such as a couple of weeks after you start, or once a month, and pick one you'll actually keep. When you sit down, ask each other what felt good, what didn't, and whether you'd like to keep the same step, change it, or add another. Some of these [relationship check-in questions to see how you're both doing](/blog/relationship-check-in) can help you look at the rest of your life together in the same conversation. When closeness has slipped again, you can simply choose a new step together.

Notice the small wins, too. The first evening you spent talking for an hour, or the hug that ended with both of you laughing, deserves a mention. Tell each other what you enjoyed.
