Intimate questions to ask your partner are questions about what helps each of you feel close. That might be feeling heard, a kind of touch, laughing together, plans you share, sex, or something neither of you has put into words yet. Ask a few when you both have time, start with something easy, and let both of you answer each one. Either of you can skip a question or stop, and nobody keeps score.

You might be here because things have felt a little less close than you'd like. Or maybe most of your conversations have turned practical, about groceries and work and who's picking up whom, and you'd like to know your partner better again. A few good questions and real attention to the answers are a good place to start.

## Why ask each other about closeness?

These questions help you find out what closeness means to your partner right now, and they let your partner find out the same about you. Two people in the same relationship can feel close in very different moments. One of you might feel nearest after a long talk in the car. The other might feel it when you're both reading in bed with your feet touching, saying nothing at all. Both of those are real closeness. Once you've heard which one matters to your partner, you know what to make room for.

It helps to treat the answers as news, even if you've been together for years. People change. What made your partner feel loved three years ago may still matter, or something else may have taken its place since a new job or a move. If you ask only to confirm what you already think, you'll hear less. Asking because you're curious leaves room to be surprised.

There are no right answers to collect. Asking is one way you look after the relationship, on an ordinary week as well as when something feels off. An answer that surprises you is useful, and so is "I'm not sure, let me think about it."

## When is a good time to ask?

Pick a time when you can both stay with the conversation. A park bench on a day off can work, and so can the couch once you've both eaten, or a morning when the first plan is still hours away. Leave it if you've just had a disagreement, when a question can sound like a complaint.

If you've been wondering how to have deep conversations with your partner, the start is usually simpler than it seems. Say what you'd like to do, and ask whether now suits them. You could say, "I found some questions about feeling close to each other, and I thought it might be nice to ask each other a few. Want to try tonight, or would another evening this week be better?"

They might want to start while you're still talking. They might say Saturday is better. If they'd rather not do it at all right now, take that as the answer. You can bring it up another week, and you don't have to talk them into it.

You don't need to have your own answers ready before you ask. You'll talk longer about each question than you expect.

## How can you start with something easy?

Begin with a question that's pleasant to answer, so you both get comfortable before anything heavier. "What's something I did recently that you liked?" works well, and so does "What made you laugh this week?" Easy questions get you both used to answering and listening, and they remind you that plenty between you is already good.

A light question also shows you the mood. If even the easy ones feel like effort tonight, you can enjoy those, stop there, and save the rest for another time. That still counts as a good conversation.

## Which intimate questions can you ask each other?

These questions cover different kinds of closeness: a moment you remember, small acts of care, touch, something you wish the other person understood, and plans you share. Sexual connection is one of them, and you can leave it out if it doesn't fit. Pick a few that feel right, and change the wording so it sounds like the two of you. Both of you answer each one you choose.

1. ### What were we doing the last time you felt close to me?

   Ask about a real moment and what was happening. Maybe it was a Sunday spent cooking together, or the night you stayed up with your partner while they worried about their sister. The details tell you more than a general answer would, because they show what closeness looks like for your partner on an ordinary day.

2. ### What's something small I do that makes you feel cared for?

   Small things are easy to stop doing when nobody mentions them. Your partner might say it's the way you warm up the car on cold mornings, or that you always ask about the friend they've been worried about. Hearing it gives you something you can keep doing, and it's lovely to hear.

3. ### What kind of touch feels good to you on an ordinary day?

   Touch that has nothing to do with sex is a kind of closeness of its own. It might be a hand on the back as you pass in the hallway, sitting close on the sofa, or a long hug before work. Some people would love more of it. Others like it only at certain times, maybe not first thing in the morning. Both are worth knowing.

4. ### What's something about you that you wish I understood better?

   This one asks your partner to share something they haven't said yet: a worry about work, something from growing up that still shapes them, or a hope they keep to themselves. Give them time. You can come back to it another day.

5. ### What's something you'd love us to do together in the next year?

   Looking ahead together is a kind of closeness too. The answer might be a trip, learning to cook food you both love, getting a dog, or simply having Friday nights at home again. Talking about it gives you something to look forward to together, even before you decide anything.

6. ### How has our sexual connection felt to you lately, and is there anything you'd like to talk about?

   Choose this one only if you'd both like to talk about sex. It asks how things have felt over a while and what each of you would like in general. It isn't about tonight, and an answer doesn't commit anyone to anything. One of you may want more, less, or something different, and those wishes can change over time. Listen to the answer as a way to know your partner better, without turning it into a request they have to meet.

## How do you take turns without keeping score?

Take turns with each question, and swap who answers first, so one of you isn't always the one opening up while the other waits. While your partner talks, your job is to understand what they're telling you.

People feel heard in different ways. One of you might feel it when the other stops what they're doing and looks over. The other might feel it when you remember what they said yesterday and ask how it went. If your partner mentions a time they didn't feel listened to, let them finish before you explain.

A good way to show you heard is to ask about one detail. "What was it about that morning?" or "What would that look like on a weeknight?" tells your partner you were paying attention, and it often brings out the part they most wanted to say.

Keep the answers from turning into a comparison. If your partner says they feel most cared for when you cook, they're telling you what they like about those evenings. If your answers to a question are very different, that's two people telling each other something true about themselves. Nobody is ahead, and nobody is behind.

Either of you can skip a question, or stop the whole conversation, at any point and without giving a reason. That goes for every question here, and especially the one about sex. Saying "I'd rather not answer that one tonight" should feel easy, and hearing it shouldn't lead to a debate. If your partner doesn't want to talk about something right now, accept that. You can let them know you'd be glad to talk about it whenever they'd like, and then leave the choice with them.

What your partner tells you belongs to them. If something they shared comes back later in an argument, the next conversation like this gets much harder to have. Keep their answers safe, and they'll find it easier to tell you more.

When you're ready to finish, end on something warm. Tell your partner one thing you liked hearing.

Once you know how you'll listen, these are the questions to take into the conversation.

## When one of you misses a kind of closeness

Sometimes an answer makes it clear that a kind of closeness has slipped away. Your partner might miss the long talks you used to have, or you might realize you miss being touched without it having to lead anywhere. Notice it, thank your partner for saying it, and don't rush to solve it right away. It can help to say what you heard and that you'd like to talk about it again soon.

When you're both ready to do something about it, the guide on [how to bring back intimacy in a relationship](/blog/how-to-bring-back-intimacy-in-a-relationship) walks through talking about what each of you misses and choosing one small next step together.

An answer can also show you that something is going really well. If you both say you love the walk you take after dinner, say so out loud. That's a small win worth celebrating, with a high five, a hug, or another walk like it.
