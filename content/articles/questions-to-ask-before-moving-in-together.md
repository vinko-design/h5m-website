Before moving in together, talk about the life you each imagine sharing. These five questions cover what you want from the move, how you expect to share costs, and the habits and space that make a home comfortable for each of you. They also give you a chance to talk about what helps you feel close.

## What is this conversation for?

It's for saying out loud what each of you expects from sharing a home, so you hear it from each other instead of guessing. Most of those expectations have never needed words. When you lived apart, it didn't matter whether Sunday was for resting or for seeing family, or whether dishes left in the sink overnight bothered anyone. Under one roof, those things come up in ordinary moments, often when one of you is tired.

Talking first won't stop every disagreement, and you'll still find things neither of you thought to mention. It gives you a starting point. When something comes up later, you can say "remember when we talked about evenings?" and pick it up as a topic you've already shared, instead of meeting it for the first time in the middle of a tense moment.

If you're the one who wants to start the conversation, you don't need to arrive with answers. Bringing the questions is enough.

## What should you ask each other?

Start with the life you each imagine, and reword the questions so they sound like the two of you.

![The five questions to ask before moving in together, printed on a card.](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-moving-in-together/check-in-questions.webp)

1. ### Why do you want to live together, and what does an ordinary week there look like to you?

   Your reasons shape a lot of small decisions. If one of you is moving in mostly to save on rent and the other sees it as the start of a shared future, a question like "should we buy a proper sofa?" means something different to each of you. Practical reasons are a normal part of the decision, so say them along with the romantic ones.

   Then describe a normal week. One of you might picture quiet weeknights at home, and the other might picture friends over for dinner most Fridays. If one of you is moving into the other's current place, talk about how it can start to feel like home for both of you, like clearing real space in the closet or choosing something for the walls together.

2. ### What costs do you expect us to share, and how do you expect to split them?

   One of you may assume rent and groceries get split equally, and the other may expect the person who earns more to pay a larger share. One of you might picture a shared pot for household costs, while the other wants to keep money mostly separate. Say what you expect and why. A wish to keep money separate might come from wanting independence or from something that happened before, and knowing which helps you both understand the answer.

   You don't need to settle numbers in this conversation. Working them out is easier once you know what each of you expects, and hearing it now means the first shared bill isn't where you discover you had different ideas.

3. ### Which of your daily habits should I know about before we share a kitchen and a bathroom?

   You'll meet each other's routines many times a day, so it helps to know them and the reasons behind them. Talk about when you usually sleep and wake up, whether you set more than one alarm, and whether you like to watch something before bed. Talk about noise, like music while you cook or calls on speaker. Talk about guests, like how much notice you'd like before a friend stays over.

   Then say what "tidy" means to you. For one person it's a clear counter and no dishes left overnight. For the other it's a place that looks fine by the weekend. Once you know what your partner prefers, a pile of mail by the door is something you can talk about, instead of assuming it means they don't care.

4. ### When will you want the place to yourself, and how will you ask for it?

   Wanting time alone at home is normal, even when you love being together. In a shared home it's easy to misread. A closed door or a quiet evening can look like being upset when your partner just needs a rest from company. One of you might need the first hour after work to be quiet, and the other might want a Saturday morning alone now and then. Say when you think you'll need it.

   Then agree on the words. Something as plain as "I need an hour on my own tonight" works. Agreeing on how to ask can make it easier to give each other space without guessing what it means.

5. ### What helps you feel close to me, and how often do you hope we'll make time for it?

   Living together can change how closeness happens. Seeing each other every day can feel close to one of you, while the other starts to miss evenings that were planned and felt special. Closeness can mean sex, and it can also mean going to bed at the same time, sitting together on the couch, or a hug in the kitchen before work. Share what you hope for, including how often, and listen to your partner's answer as a way to know them better. Hopes like these can change after you move in, so this is a good one to come back to.

## How do you have the talk?

Pick a time when neither of you is rushed or tired, and a setting that feels easy. A long walk works well, because you're side by side and nobody has to hold eye contact the whole time. A kitchen table works too, with something to drink and your phones in another room.

Take turns going first, so one person isn't always the one who speaks first. Let each of you answer fully before you move on, and ask a follow-up when an answer surprises you. A good one is "what would an ordinary Tuesday look like for you?", because it turns a general preference into a picture you can both see.

If you've already moved in, the same questions still work, and you'll have real examples to talk about. If a question needs more thought, say so and agree on when you'll come back to it. The talk is worth having even if a few answers stay open for now.

## How do you keep it a conversation?

Give both of you the same room to speak and the same patience to finish a thought. You're trying to understand how each of you wants to live, so answers work best as descriptions of what you need, instead of judgments about the other person.

Shared work around the home is an easy place for this to slip. If you find yourselves adding up who did what when you stayed at each other's places, bring it back to the question in front of you: what would feel fair and comfortable to each of you in the home you're about to share. Rather than keeping a tally, talk about what each of you expects and how you might share the work.

If you hear yourself starting a sentence with "you always" or "you never," pause and say what happened and what you'd like to change. Then ask your partner what would work better for them, and listen to their answer.

Once you're living together, looking back now and then helps you both see how the shared life is going and what you'd like to change. These [relationship check-in questions](/blog/relationship-check-in) are made for that, at whatever interval suits the two of you.
