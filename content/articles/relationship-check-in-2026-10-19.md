A relationship check-in is one regular sitting, weekly or monthly, where the two of you talk about how things are going. Keep it to a few questions: how the days actually went, what each of you noticed, and what you want to pay attention to next. When you've both answered them, you stop. The questions are below, along with how to keep the talk calm and two-sided.

## What is a relationship check-in for?

It's a set time to talk about the two of you, so that talk doesn't depend on finding a spare moment. It's easy for a week to get away from you. Work and errands fill every conversation, and you can go a long time sorting out who's picking up what without once asking how things are going between you.

A check-in gives that question a place. Small things get said while they're still small, and good things get noticed instead of slipping past. It's just as useful in a good stretch as in a rough one. In a good stretch, it's where you notice what's working and celebrate it. In a rough one, it's a calm place to start from, so the talk doesn't only happen when someone's already upset.

Aim for one honest look at how things went and one or two things to try next. That's enough for one sitting.

## How often should you do a relationship check-in?

Weekly or monthly, whichever the two of you will actually keep. Both work, and they feel a little different.

A weekly check-in is shorter and fresher. You still remember Tuesday, so it's easier to talk about specific moments than a general mood. It suits busy stretches, like the first months after moving in together, when a lot changes at once and small frictions can pile up fast.

A monthly check-in gives you more distance. You can see whether something was a one-off or keeps coming back. It suits couples who already talk a lot day to day and don't want another weekly ritual. The tradeoff is memory, since a month is long enough to forget the details, so the note-jotting tip further down matters more.

Pick one and try it for three or four rounds before you judge it, even if the first one feels stiff.

## How do you keep a check-in to one sitting?

Give it a regular slot, a calm moment, and an end. The same day and time each week or month means nobody has to open with "can we talk?", which can make the other person brace for bad news. A calm moment means not when one of you is worn out or still annoyed about something from that afternoon. Sunday evening after dinner, a slow weekend morning, or a walk around the block all work. Put your phones away, or at least face down.

About half an hour is plenty. A weekly one can be shorter. The sitting ends when you've both answered the questions. If one topic turns out to need a longer talk, say so, agree on when you'll have it, and leave it for then. That keeps the check-in something neither of you dreads, and it keeps the bigger topic from being squeezed into the last ten minutes.

During the week, if something comes up that you want to talk about, write a word or two down, on your phone or on paper, and bring it to the check-in. You don't have to raise it in the moment, and you won't forget it. Keep it to a reminder, though. A long list saved up for Sunday turns the sitting into a hearing.

If you're the one who wants to start, suggest it lightly. Something like "I'd like us to have a regular half hour to talk about how we're doing. Nothing's wrong, I just think it'd be nice." Let your partner pick the time, and offer to try it once and see how it feels. If they'd like to add a question of their own, even better. It's a habit for both of you, so it helps if it feels like theirs too.

## What questions should you ask in a relationship check-in?

Ask about how the days actually went, what each of you noticed, and what you want to pay attention to next. These six check-in questions for couples cover it. Take turns, and both of you answer each one.

1. How did the days actually go for you since last time?
2. What was a good moment, big or small?
3. What did you notice about us, something that went well or something that drifted?
4. Was anything harder than it needed to be?
5. What do you want us to pay attention to before next time?
6. What's one small thing we could do about it?

Use the same questions for a monthly check-in. "Since last time" just covers more ground. The good moment comes second on purpose, because it sets the tone for the rest.

**How the days actually went.** The word "actually" matters here. Compare what happened with what you hoped for. Maybe you meant to have two evenings to yourselves and had none, or you both said you'd get to bed earlier and only one of you did. Say it plainly, without blame. It's information, and you can do something with information.

**What each of you noticed.** Each of you answers from your own view, and you'll often notice different things. One of you felt the week was busy but close, and the other felt you barely saw each other. Neither of you is wrong, and the gap between those two pictures is often the most useful thing in the whole talk. Keep it to what you noticed and how it felt. "I noticed we didn't eat together much this week, and I missed it" lands very differently from "You're never home for dinner."

**What to pay attention to next.** Settle on one or two things, three at most. That might be something to protect, like the Saturday morning walk that clearly helps, or something to change, like moving the gym night so it doesn't take your only free evening together. The last question makes it concrete: one small thing you'll try before the next sitting. Next time, start by asking how it went.

## How do you keep a check-in a conversation instead of a scorecard?

Both of you speak, and nobody keeps score. Take turns on each question, and let the other person finish before you answer. If your partner says something you see differently, ask about it first. "What was that like for you?" gets you further than a correction.

Keeping score is the quickest way to lose what a check-in is for. It looks like tallying who did more, or arriving with a list of the other person's misses. The sitting turns into something to win, and whoever keeps losing stops wanting to come. Talk about the two of you and what you're trying to do together, and leave the ledger out of it.

It helps to treat what you notice as shared. "We had no evenings together this week" is something you can both look at and do something about. "You booked every evening" is something one person has to defend.

Some check-ins will be harder than others. If something stings, it's fine to say so and take a minute. If a talk starts to heat up, pause, say you'd like to come back to it, and then come back to it at a time you agree on. If the same hard topic keeps returning and you can't move it on your own, talking to a couples counsellor together is a reasonable next step. A check-in is a regular habit for staying close, and it doesn't have to carry everything.

Finish on something good. A thank-you for something specific from the week, or one thing you're looking forward to, is enough. Those are the moments worth a high five, and noticing them together is a big part of why the check-in is worth keeping.
