To make time for your partner, start by finding out what time together means to each of you, because one of you may be happy sharing a room while the other wants real attention. Then pick one realistic window in the schedules you actually have, agree on how you'll use it, and look at the plan again when life changes. Start with a plan you both want and leave room for time on your own.

If you've noticed you're not spending enough time together, you probably didn't decide that. Work, errands, and tiredness fill the evenings, and time together becomes whatever is left at ten at night. You can start with one honest conversation and one small change you both want.

## What does time together mean to each of you?

Two people can spend the same evening on the same sofa and remember it differently. One of you had a lovely night: you were both home, a show was on, one of you was answering emails. The other went to bed feeling a bit lonely, because nobody really talked. Neither of you is wrong. Being near each other and paying attention to each other are both real kinds of time together, and they meet different needs.

So before you look for more hours, find out which kind each of you is missing. One way in is to ask about a real moment instead of the idea in general. You could ask, "When did you last feel like we really spent time together?" and then answer it yourself too. Talk about what made that moment count. Was it the conversation, the activity, the fact that phones were away, or just being in the same place after a long day?

Your answers might not match, and that's useful. If one of you counts a quiet evening at home and the other counts a walk where you actually talk, you can talk about whether you'd like more of either kind.

You might find it helpful to give the two kinds your own names, like "sofa time" and "just us time." Then either of you can ask for one without explaining it from the start every time.

## How do you say what you miss without it sounding like a complaint?

What you miss is a wish about the future, so say it that way. "You never make time for me anymore" tells your partner they've been failing. "I miss our Saturday mornings at the market, and I'd love to bring something like that back" tells them what you'd enjoy. The second version gives them something to say yes to.

Talk about one or two specific things instead of a general feeling of distance. "I miss cooking dinner together and talking while we do it" is easier to act on than "I want us to be closer." It also keeps you away from counting. Once you start listing how many evenings went to work or friends, the conversation turns into a score, and nobody wants to defend their week against a tally.

Timing matters as much as wording. Choose a moment when neither of you is rushing out the door or already upset about something else. A walk, washing up together, or a slow weekend breakfast might give you space to talk. You could open with something like, "Can we talk this weekend about how we spend our evenings? Nothing's wrong, I'd just like more of them with you." Your partner might say this weekend is fine, or they might want to talk about it right away. Either is good.

Then ask what your partner misses, and listen to the answer before you respond. They may miss something you didn't expect, like lazy mornings, or they may say they'd like more time to themselves. That's part of the picture too, and it belongs in the plan you make together.

## Where can shared time come from in a busy week?

Start with the week you really have, not the one you wish you had. Sit down together and look at next week side by side, in whatever calendar, notebook, or note you already use. Look for the places where you're both home, both awake, and not already committed. Look for a small opening that you might otherwise overlook.

Places where time together often hides:

- A chore you could do together, like cooking or folding laundry
- The first or last stretch of the day, before phones come out
- An errand or commute you could share
- An evening you free up by saying no to one optional plan
- A different way to split chores so you both finish sooner

Pick one window to start with. You don't need five. A realistic window is one that still works in an ordinary, slightly messy week. If you work different shifts, it might be breakfast on the one morning you're both off. If you have small children, it might be the half hour after they're asleep, or a walk with the stroller where you actually talk.

Saying no is part of finding time, and it's easier when you decide together what's worth giving up. Maybe one of you skips a weekly optional meeting, or you both agree that one night a week has no plans with anyone else. Pay attention to rest as well. If you're both exhausted, a window that needs a lot of energy won't last. Shared rest, like a slow Sunday morning with coffee and no plans, counts as time together.

## What will you do with the time you find?

Agree ahead of time on what the window is for. A free evening can fill up with phones, logistics, and whatever needs doing around the house. You don't need a detailed program. You just need to know whether this is sofa time or just us time, and roughly what you'll do.

Small rituals are a good fit for busy weeks because they don't take much planning. A coffee together before the day starts, a walk after dinner, or ten minutes at the end of the day where each of you hears about the other's day can give you a chance to pay attention to each other. Choose something you both enjoy and might want to repeat.

You can also bring each other into things you already do. If one of you goes to the gym, cooks on Sundays, or does grocery shopping, the other can join some of the time. A hobby, a show you only watch together, or a regular coffee spot works the same way. It can turn an activity you would do separately into time side by side, if you both enjoy it.

Being apart doesn't have to mean being out of touch. A message at lunch about something specific from your morning, a call on the way home, or a photo of something that made you think of your partner can help you stay in touch on busy days. A specific message can feel personal. "Saw a dog in a raincoat and thought of you" feels like attention. Choose the kind of message that feels natural to you both.

One thing is worth keeping out of the window if you can: household planning. If your time together turns into talking about bills and dentist appointments, it becomes a meeting. Give those things their own moment so the window stays something you both look forward to. And if the plan you picked stops being enjoyable, change it. It's meant to be time you want, not one more thing on the list.

## Where does time on your own fit?

Time on your own, or with friends, is part of a full life, and it gives each of you things to bring back to the relationship. Making more time for your partner shouldn't mean giving all of it up. A good plan protects some of each.

Two people may want different amounts of shared time. One of you might feel recharged by an evening together, while the other needs a few quiet hours alone after a busy day. Neither of those is a problem to fix. They're two needs to fit into the same week.

Talk about what each of you needs to feel rested, and build that into the plan instead of treating it as whatever's left over. That could be one evening that belongs to each of you alone, and one that belongs to both of you. Sofa time can help here too. Reading in the same room or cooking while the other relaxes nearby can give one person company and the other some quiet, both at once.

If you're the one who wants more time together, try not to treat your partner's time alone as time taken away from you. And if you're the one who wants more space, it helps to say clearly when you'll be back and what you're looking forward to doing together. That gives your partner a clearer idea of when you might spend time together.

## How do you come back to the plan when life changes?

Life changes, so decide together when you'll look at yours again. Pick whatever interval suits you, like every couple of weeks or once a month, or simply the next time something changes. Keep it short. Talk about what worked, what didn't, and what you'd like to try differently.

Some weeks won't go to plan. Someone gets sick or a deadline takes up the evening. A missed evening may tell you something about the week or the window you chose. If the same window keeps getting filled by other things, it might be in the wrong place. Move it instead of trying harder to hold on to it. What you shouldn't do is keep a list of missed evenings and bring it up later.

Bigger changes call for a fresh look at the whole plan. A new job, a move, exams, a new baby, or a partner working away for a while can all change which windows exist and what each of you needs from them. When that happens, go back to the first question, what time together means to each of you right now, and build from there.

When the plan works, say so. "I really loved our walk on Thursday" is a small thing to say, and it tells your partner what's worth keeping. That's a high-five moment worth noticing together.
