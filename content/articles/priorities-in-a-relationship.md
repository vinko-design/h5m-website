Priorities in a relationship are the things each of you gives time and energy to, plus the things you've decided matter to you as a couple. They can differ without either person being wrong or caring less. Sometimes a difference means a clash over a weekend or a budget; other times, one of you wants help making progress on a career change or getting fit. Start by saying what matters to each of you right now, then choose one thing that needs support or a trade-off and agree on a small next step you'll look at again later.

## What are priorities in a relationship?

A priority is something you choose to spend time and energy on, especially when you don't have enough of either for everything. For each of you, that might be your career or your health, like sleeping better or getting fit again after an injury. It might be a sport you play, studying for a qualification, time with friends, or a quiet evening alone to recharge. As a couple, you'll also have things you're building together, like saving money, making your home feel right, seeing each other's families, or having evenings where you actually talk about your day.

Some of the things people say matter most work a little differently. Respect, for example, describes how you treat each other while you make room for everything else. It shows up when you take your partner's training seriously, or ask before you fill their Saturday with plans. You don't have to rank it against health or a career. Staying close works in a similar way. For one of you, that might mean a quick call at lunch. For the other, it might mean a longer talk after dinner. Independence might mean keeping up your friendships and having some time to yourself. Shared values show up in the plans you choose together.

It helps to look at each priority from a few angles. Some are personal, some are shared, and some are competing at the moment. Those views overlap. Your partner's work qualification is personal to them, and once you've both agreed to make room for it, supporting it becomes a shared priority too.

"Competing" describes a situation where two things want the same time, money, or energy. That can happen inside one person's week. Say you're studying for a work qualification and also training with your football team, and there aren't enough evenings for both, so you skip one training night a week until the exam. That's your call, but it's still worth talking through. Your partner might see effects you don't, like you coming home tired or weekends filling up with revision. Talking about it doesn't mean they have to give something up as well. It can also happen between you. Maybe one of you wants to save hard for a house deposit while the other wants to book a summer holiday.

Everyday things deserve a place in this too. When [Pew Research Center surveyed married and cohabiting adults in the US](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2019/11/06/how-married-and-cohabiting-adults-see-their-relationships/), it asked how satisfied they were with how chores were divided, how their partner balanced work and personal life, how well they communicated, and their sex life. Among parents with children under 18 at home, it also asked about their partner's approach to parenting. Any of those can become a priority you name together.

No list of top priorities in a relationship fits every couple. Two people can put the same things in a different order, and both can be reasonable.

## Why do priorities change over time?

Life keeps changing what needs your attention. A new job, exam season, a parent who needs care, or moving in together can push something to the top of your list for a few months. Something that came first last spring might need to wait for a while, and that can be completely fair.

When the change happens quietly, the other person has to guess what it means. If you're about to get busier, tell your partner early and be specific:

"Work's going to be heavy until the end of the month. I'll probably miss our Thursday dinners, but I want to keep Sunday mornings for us."

That one sentence tells your partner what's changing, roughly how long it will last, and what you're keeping. Without it, they may assume the relationship matters less.

"Busy for a while" is easier to live with when "a while" has an end, or at least a date when you'll talk about it again. If the busy stretch keeps getting longer, that deserves its own conversation.

## What does support look like for each of you?

Asking for "more support" is a start, but your partner can't do much with it. A request they can act on names an action and a time. "Can you cook on Tuesdays and Thursdays while I'm studying?" gives your partner something they can say yes to, or a starting point if they'd like to suggest a different plan.

[One study of couples](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.609232/full) describes perceived partner responsiveness as the sense that your partner understands your needs and responds in a supportive way. That's a helpful way to think about feeling prioritized. It has two parts: your partner understands what you need, and they respond to it. So when you ask for support, tell your partner what you need and what kind of response would feel helpful.

Do the same for the relationship itself. These are shared priorities too. When you try the paper exercise, include them alongside your individual ones. Each of you can say what you believe keeps it going. For one person, that might be a phone-free dinner most nights. For the other, it might be a text when they're running late, or one evening out every couple of weeks. You may find you care about different things. One of you might feel cared for when plans are kept, while the other feels cared for when plans can change easily. Neither is wrong, and it's much easier to make room for both once you've each said it out loud.

## How do you support each other's goals and still make time for the two of you?

You can make room for what each person wants to achieve while still protecting time for the two of you. Helping each other move forward can also feel good for both of you. A win one of you worked hard for is often one you both get to enjoy.

Sometimes nothing is clashing at all. One of you is just stuck. Maybe you've been trying to get fitter for months and keep losing the routine, or you're applying for jobs and hearing nothing back. Saying "I'm finding this hard, and I'd like your help" is a fair thing to ask. Your partner might offer to come along to the gym on Mondays, or read through your applications before you send them.

When a goal takes real time, name it together. Say what help would make a difference, and what it might cost the relationship for now. Maybe one of you is training for a race, so Saturday mornings are gone for three months. Then agree on what you'll keep for the two of you during that time. A small slot you both protect is enough, like a walk on Sunday afternoons or a late breakfast after the long run.

Starting a business is a goal that needs a careful talk. In the early months it can take evenings, weekends, and savings, and give little back. It might help you both later, or it might not, and nobody can promise which. Before you start, talk about whose idea it is and how much each of you wants it. Agree what help your partner is happy to give, and make sure they're choosing it freely. Be clear about what it costs right now. Set some limits, like how much money goes in or which evenings stay free, and pick a time to look at it again. A possible payoff later can be a good reason to try, as long as it doesn't become a reason for one of you to keep giving more without limit.

Be honest when a goal starts to take more than you agreed. "I'm happy to cover the mornings, but I'm missing our evenings too" tells your partner what needs adjusting without dismissing their goal.

Celebrating each other's wins is part of the support. Say your partner finishes a part-time diploma they've worked at for months while you handled extra dinners. That's a win for both of you. Give them a high five, and then whatever comes next, maybe a hug or a dinner out to celebrate together.

## Which commitments stay firm, and which can move?

Many clashes get easier once you've agreed which plans are firm and which can move. A firm plan is one where changing it would really hurt, like a birthday dinner, a promise to come to a medical appointment, or a trip you've both paid for. A flexible plan is one you both enjoy but can shift, like your usual movie night.

Talk about this before the next clash, so neither of you has to decide in the moment. You might be surprised. Something your partner treats as easy to move might be the one plan you were counting on all week.

When a flexible plan has to move, offer a new time right away. "Can we do Saturday instead of Friday?" feels very different from "I can't make it" with nothing after it. Rescheduling tells your partner the plan still matters to you. Cancelling with no new time leaves them wondering whether it does.

If a firm plan has to change, treat it as a bigger deal. Say sorry, explain why, and ask what would make it right.

A compromise doesn't always mean you each give up half. Sometimes it means taking turns. This month, you visit one partner's family for Sunday lunch, and next month you keep a quiet weekend at home. You both need to accept the trade, and the same person shouldn't be the one giving way every time.

## How do you talk through your priorities together?

Try using a few pieces of paper to start the conversation. If you'd rather skip the paper, you can talk through the same steps.

Each of you takes six sticky notes, or cuts a sheet of A4 paper into six pieces. Write one priority on each piece. Include your personal ones, like "study for my exam," and ones you share, like a project you're working on together or something that keeps the relationship going, such as "Sunday walks together." Six is just the number of spaces. If fewer things are on your mind, leave some blank.

1. Write your personal and shared priorities on your own.
2. Sort them by how much attention each needs right now.
3. Lay both sets out and explain your order.
4. Agree on your shared pieces, and add any you both missed.
5. Compare shared and personal pieces, then choose one that needs support or a trade-off.
6. Agree on one next step and when to look again.

Doing the first part alone means you each write what's true for you, without adjusting to the other person. When you compare, ask what each piece means before you decide it's shared. Money belongs here, but turn it into the actual aim, like "pay off the credit card" or "save for a home." If you both just write "money," one of you might mean paying off debt while the other means saving for a holiday.

The order shows where effort needs to go at the moment, and it isn't a measure of how much anything or anyone matters. A piece can sit lower because it's going well and needs less effort, like the Sunday walks you never miss. Write activities and goals, never people. Your partner isn't one of the pieces, so nobody ends up placed below a job. Two pieces can share a spot, and you don't need to end up with the same order.

If a priority really is shared, you can stack your two pieces together or write one new piece for both of you. Do that only when you both agree. Personal pieces stay on the table where you can both see them, even if only one of you wrote them.

Then move the pieces around together and look for one place that needs attention. It might be a goal one of you wants help with, or a shared plan and a personal goal that want the same evening or the same money. Pick one, and talk about that one only.

Here's how that might sound with the holiday and the house deposit.

"I'd really like to book a holiday this summer. We've both been working so hard."

"I want that too. I'm just worried about the deposit. If we spend a lot now, we'll be another year away."

"What if we kept it small? A few days somewhere we can drive to, with a set budget."

"That could work. Can we agree on the budget tonight and still put the usual amount into savings this month?"

"Deal. Let's look at it again at the end of the month."

Your partner might not agree right away. They might want to talk it through more, or they might have a better idea. That's fine, because the aim is a decision you both can live with, and a first answer isn't always it.

Make the next step something you can actually do in the week you have. Name who does it, when, and how much time or energy it takes. "Agree on a holiday budget tonight" fits into one evening, while "sort out our finances" doesn't. If you're both tired, one evening together is a better step than three that won't happen.

Treat the next step as something to try, rather than a promise that one conversation will settle everything. [A study that followed newlywed couples](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4852543/) found that communication and satisfaction tended to go together, but communication didn't reliably predict how satisfied couples felt later. A good talk gives you one thing to try, and the next few weeks show you whether it helped.

## When should you come back to what you agreed?

Pick a time that suits the two of you. You might look back each week, once a month, or when the busy stretch should be over. Both of you should know when you'll return to it.

When you sit down again, keep it simple. Did the next step happen, and did it help? Talk through anything that needs to change. If the week didn't go to plan, say so without blame, and adjust the step. A plan that didn't work is something to learn from, and you can try again with a better idea. If you'd like a wider format for that conversation, use these [relationship check-in questions](/blog/relationship-check-in) to look at how the rest of your life together is going.

If it did work, say so too. Noticing that the two of you kept your Sunday mornings through a hard month is worth a moment of celebration.

## What if you feel like you're not a priority?

Maybe your priorities don't seem to matter much to your partner. Or maybe it's bigger than one of you, and the relationship itself isn't getting enough time or attention. Either way, feeling like you're not a priority in the relationship hurts, and it's worth raising.

Start with what you've noticed and what you'd like, and leave out the verdict. "Our plans have fallen through a lot lately, and I miss you. Could we pick one evening this week that we both keep?" names the problem and gives your partner something concrete to answer. "You never make time for me" assumes you know why they're doing it.

Then listen. Your partner might be more stretched than you knew, or they might feel the same distance and not have known how to say it. If neither of you has been giving the relationship enough time, talk about it as something you're both responsible for: "We've both been so busy. What would help us get some time back?"

It's often easier to start with one manageable request and build from there. One evening a week that you both keep is a good first step. Once that's holding, you can ask for more. Starting small is a way to begin, and it doesn't mean settling for less forever.

Instead of testing whether your partner notices or quietly counting every missed plan, say what you need directly so they have a fair chance to respond.

You also don't have to stay quiet when the same thing keeps happening. If you've asked clearly, agreed on a change, and it still doesn't happen, say that plainly. Your needs count, and you're allowed to take them seriously, even when there isn't a quick answer.
