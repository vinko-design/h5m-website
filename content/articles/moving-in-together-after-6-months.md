Moving in together after 6 months can be right for both of you, and so can waiting a while longer. No standard number of months decides it. What decides it is what each of you would need to be true before you share a home, so each of you writes that down, you compare the two lists, and you choose a next step that fits both.

Maybe the idea came up over dinner, and one of you lit up while the other went a little quiet. Or maybe you're both excited, and a friend asked whether it's a bit soon. Either way, six months is the timeline in front of you, and the decision belongs to both of you.

## Is six months too soon to move in together?

On its own, six months doesn't make a move too soon or too late. The number tells you how long you've been together. It doesn't tell you what you've learned about each other in that time, or what each of you wants from a shared home.

Two couples who met on the same day can be in very different places by now. One pair might have spent most weekends at each other's apartments and already know how the other likes their mornings. Another pair might mostly meet for dinner and a film, and have never spent an ordinary Tuesday under the same roof. Neither couple is behind. They'd simply write different things when they think about what needs to happen before a move.

So when people ask how long before you move in together, or how long to date first, a count of months gives them a number without telling them anything about their own relationship. A more useful question is what would need to be true for you, and for your partner, before you share a home. You can find that answer, and you find it by asking each other.

## Why do you each want to move now?

Start with your reasons, because they shape everything else you'll write. Each of you says why you'd like to move now, in your own words. One of you might want to stop saying goodbye at the end of every evening. The other might have a lease ending in the spring, or be tired of paying rent on a place they barely sleep in. Those reasons can sit side by side. A practical reason is a normal part of this decision, and your partner will understand your plans better if they hear it alongside the romantic one.

Saying goodnight at the door is one of those evenings.

![A couple laughs while saying goodnight at an apartment door, one partner holding car keys and a coat.](/blog/moving-in-together-after-6-months/goodnight.webp)

People move in for a mix of reasons. In [a study that asked cohabiting couples to rank their reasons for living together](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2743430/), the reason people put first varied. Some chose wanting more time with their partner, some chose that the move made financial sense, some wanted to test the relationship before marriage, and some didn't believe in marriage. Most put time with their partner first, but plenty didn't. It's one group of couples, asked after they'd already been living together for a while, so treat it as a picture of how varied reasons can be. Yours might look a lot like your partner's, or they might be quite different, and either is useful to hear.

Listen to your partner's reason as news about what they're hoping for. If you catch yourself weighing whether their reason is good enough, ask what the move would change for them instead.

## What would need to be true for you before you share a home?

Each of you answers for yourself. Write what you'd need in sentences that start with "I," and leave your partner's habits out of it. "I'd want to have spent a full week at your place first" belongs on your list. "You'd need to stop leaving dishes in the sink" is a request about them, and it belongs in a different conversation.

A few prompts can help you get started:

- What I'd want us to have done together first
- What I'd want us to have talked through
- What I'd need from the home itself
- What I'd want to keep from my life now
- What would help the date feel right to me

Your answers can be small and concrete. "I'd want us to have spent a long weekend away together, somewhere that isn't either of our places." "I'd need a desk where I can take work calls." "I'd want to keep Thursday nights for my climbing group." "I'd want you to have met my sister before I tell my parents." "I'd want to feel settled about the money side, because I'd be giving up a cheap room."

Feelings count as answers too. "I'd want to stop feeling nervous when I picture it" is a real item, and writing it down gives you something to ask yourself about. Some things on your list will already be true. Others might need a conversation, a visit, or a bit more time. Write them all down. If you can't think of anything you'd need first, that's an answer as well.

## How do you compare your answers?

Either of you can suggest it. You might say, "I keep thinking about us living together. Before we pick a date, could we each write down what we'd want in place first, and then compare? Maybe Sunday morning?" Your partner might say they already know their answers and want to talk right now, and if you both have the time, there's no reason to wait. They might also want a few days to think. Agree on when, and keep to it.

1. On your own, write your reason for moving now at the top of a page.
2. Under it, list what would need to be true for you before you share a home.
3. Next to each item, note whether it's already true or still to come.
4. Sit down together and read your reasons to each other first.
5. Take turns reading one item at a time from your lists.
6. Put both lists side by side and look at where they match and where they differ.

Writing separately keeps each list honest to the person who wrote it, without either of you adjusting to the other's answers. Reading your reasons first gives every item that follows some context. When it's your turn to listen, say back what you heard, something like "So you'd want a desk for your calls, and right now there's no room for one at my place?" Then ask what it would look like once it's sorted.

Matches are a natural place to start. If you both wrote something like "spend a full week living at one place," you've already found a first step. Differences tell you something too. Say one list has three items still to come and the other has none. That means one of you has more you'd like in place before a date is set, and it's better to learn that now than on moving day. Go through each item that's still to come and ask what would make it true. Some you can do together, like trying a week at one apartment. Others only the person who wrote them can answer, like how they'd feel about telling their parents.

Be careful the comparison doesn't turn into a grade for the relationship or for either of you. Each list describes what one person would need, and a longer list simply shows that person has more they'd like in place, which helps you both plan. Writing and comparing like this is one way to look after your relationship while you make a big decision. On a week when the move feels like an easy yes, it lets each of you hear what the other is counting on. When one of you feels unsure, it gives that feeling something specific to point at.

## What can you decide once you've compared?

Choose a next step that fits both lists. If everything on both pages is already true, moving on the date you've been talking about may be the natural next step, and you can start planning it.

If some items are still to come, agree which of them have to happen before the move, and who does what. That might sound like, "We'll spend the first week of next month at your place, and I'll find out what it costs to end my lease early." Then pick a time to look at the lists again, whenever suits you both. Deciding to wait is still a decision, and it comes with a clear plan for what happens next.

A week at one apartment is one of those in-between steps.

![A woman unpacks a weekend bag into a cleared drawer at her partner's apartment while he hands her hangers.](/blog/moving-in-together-after-6-months/weekend-bag.webp)

If one of you wants to move now and the other wants a couple of things in place first, look at those things together. Sometimes it comes down to one or two concrete items you can plan around. Sometimes it changes the plan itself, like choosing a new place together instead of one of you moving into the other's current apartment.

Plans can slip. If the week at each other's place keeps getting pushed back, say so and pick a new date. When an item becomes true, mention it, because each one is progress you both get to see. And when the last things on both lists are done, or you carry the first box through the door, that's a high-five moment. Celebrate it however suits you, with an actual high five, a takeaway dinner on the floor, or a walk around your new neighbourhood.

## Where do money, chores, and daily habits fit in?

Some of your items will probably be about rent, cleaning, sleep, or time alone. Those deserve their own conversation, and these [questions to ask before moving in together](/blog/questions-to-ask-before-moving-in-together) walk you through it, from how you'd split costs to when each of you will want the place to yourself. If that talk hasn't happened yet, you can put it on your lists as one of the things to do before the move.
