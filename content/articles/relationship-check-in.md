A relationship check-in is a regular conversation where the two of you talk about how things have actually been going, what each of you has noticed, and what you'd like to look after next. You decide how often it happens. A short set of questions gives it a shape and a natural end. Here are five that work, and how to set the time up so it stays easy to come back to.

## Why have a regular check-in at all?

It gives the things that matter between you a calm, regular slot, so you talk about how you're doing on an ordinary day instead of waiting for a bad one to force it.

On a normal day, most of what you say to each other is practical: whether anyone called the plumber, when the train gets in, what's for dinner. The nice moments go past without comment. Small irritations don't get mentioned either, so they build up and come out later over something unrelated. A check-in is where both get said while they're still small.

It's also how you spot drift between the life you're aiming for and the one you've had lately. Maybe you'd meant to have more evenings out, and a busy month swallowed them. Maybe one of you wanted to get back into running and it hasn't happened once. Nothing changes until one of you says it, and the fix is often smaller than you'd think, like a different night or a chore swapped between you.

And it's where good news gets noticed. When you pull off the thing you said you'd try, mentioning it next time lets you both see you're getting somewhere. Those are the wins worth a high five, and they slip by if nobody stops to look.

## When and how often should you check in?

Pick an interval you can keep up easily and a time when you're both relaxed.

That could be weekly, every other week, or monthly. It helps to tie it to something that already repeats, like Sunday breakfast or the first weekend of the month. The right gap is the one you'll still be keeping in three months, so start with whatever feels easy and adjust once you've tried it a few times.

Then give it a regular slot. Choose a time when you're both rested and neither of you has somewhere to be, like a slow breakfast or a quiet evening once the kitchen's tidy, and keep to it so you're not negotiating a date every time. If you've just had a disagreement, move it to another day. A check-in goes better when you start it on decent terms.

Make the setting easy. Sit somewhere comfortable, get a drink, and leave the phones in another room. If facing each other across a table feels like an interview, take the questions on a walk. Side by side, some of them are easier to answer.

Keep a short list between check-ins of anything you'd like to bring up. A few words are enough to remind you when the time comes, and nothing gets lost just because it came up on a busy Tuesday.

Finish when the questions are finished, even if it went quickly. A check-in that doesn't drag on is one you'll both agree to next time.

## What should you ask in a relationship check-in?

Ask the same five questions each time, in the same order, and let both of you answer each one before you move on. Long lists of couples check-in questions are good for browsing, but in one conversation they turn into homework. These five check-in questions for couples start with something good, look at your days and at the two of you, and finish with a plan.

![The five relationship check-in questions from this page, printed on a card.](/blog/relationship-check-in/check-in-questions.webp)

1. ### What was a good moment between us since last time?

   This one gets you both talking about something easy. Make it specific: the evening you cooked together, the message that made you laugh in a meeting, how they handled your mom's visit. Small is fine. Starting here reminds you that plenty went right since your last check-in, and the rest of the talk feels lighter for it.

2. ### What's been taking up most of your energy lately?

   This one gives each of you room to talk about your own days. It might be work, sleep, money, a friend who's struggling, or a health thing you keep putting off. Say it plainly, even if it seems minor. In a [diary study of 55 married couples](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00401/full), spelling out what had been stressful went hand in hand with both partners seeing the other's support as more responsive. Your partner can only help with what they know about.

3. ### When did we feel close, and when did we feel distant?

   This one is about the two of you together. Keep to what happened rather than what it says about anyone: "we kept going to bed at different times," or "we've been good at Saturday mornings lately." Give the close moments as much attention as the distant ones. If the same thing keeps coming up, check-in after check-in, that's probably the one worth working on.

4. ### What would help you most in the days ahead?

   This one is where you look after each other. It asks what would help in the days ahead, so the answer is something your partner can actually do: take the Thursday school run so you can stay late, ask how the interview went, leave you an hour to yourself on Saturday. Hearing it directly saves you both a lot of guessing.

5. ### What's one thing we'll look after before our next check-in?

   This one turns all of that into one plan. Settle on a single thing, modest enough that it'll really happen, like a walk together on Saturday or going to bed at the same time twice. When it does happen, mention it at the start of your next check-in. That's progress you both get to see, and it gives the first question an easy answer.

Treat the five as a starting point. Once you've done this a few times, reword them until they sound like the two of you.

## How do you keep it a conversation instead of a scorecard?

Give each other equal room, and talk about what happened rather than who did more.

When your partner is answering, let them finish. Then say back what you heard and ask one thing about it before you add your side, something like "So that deadline's been hanging over you for days?" It's a small habit, and it changes how the whole talk feels. A [seven-day diary study of 99 couples](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.609232/full) found that people tended to be more satisfied with their relationship when they felt their partner responded well to them, both on a given day and overall. That doesn't prove a check-in will make you happier, but it's a good reason to make sure you both come away feeling heard.

Swap who goes first each time, so neither of you is always the one opening up or always the one responding.

Leave tallies out of it. Counting who did more of the chores, or who was late more often, turns a check-in sour fast. Workloads go up and down. If one of you has been carrying more lately, treat it as something to plan around together, and don't turn it into a debt. Nobody's handing out marks here.

Sometimes a subject lands harder than either of you expected. If one of you gets so overwhelmed that it's hard to listen or speak clearly, stop for a while. The Gottman Institute suggests [taking a break of at least 20 minutes and no more than 24 hours](https://www.gottman.com/blog/does-flooding-play-a-role-in-your-perpetual-conflict/), doing something calming in the meantime, and agreeing ahead of time on a phrase either of you can use to call that break. Come back to the questions when you're both settled. If the subject turns out to need more than one conversation, give it its own time on another day.

Expect the first one or two to feel a little formal. Once you both know the questions and know it ends on a plan, it gets easier to start.
