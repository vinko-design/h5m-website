A relationship check-in is a regular talk, weekly or monthly, where the two of you look back at how things have been going and pick one thing to pay attention to next. It goes best with a handful of questions you ask in the same order each time, so you both know where the talk is heading and when it's done. Below are six of them, and how to set the time up so it stays easy to keep doing.

## What is a relationship check-in for?

It's a set time to talk about your life together while things are fine, so you're not only talking about it when something's gone wrong.

Most weeks get away from people. You talk every day, but a lot of it is logistics: who's picking up what, who's home when, what's for dinner. The small good things go by without anyone mentioning them, and the small annoyances pile up until one of them comes out sideways on a random Tuesday. A check-in gives both of those somewhere to go.

It's also a chance to adjust a little. Maybe you wanted more evenings together and it's been mostly screens and early nights. Maybe one of you has a heavy stretch at work coming up and you'd both like to plan around it. Looking back at the last week or month together and choosing one small change is far easier than trying to fix everything at once, or waiting for a bad week to force the conversation.

## When should you have a check-in?

Pick a time when you're both fed and not in a hurry, and keep it in the same slot each week or month. A Sunday afternoon or an evening after dinner is a good place to start. Avoid late at night, when you're both running on empty, and avoid the hour after an argument. A check-in goes better when neither of you is already upset.

Put your phones in another room and turn the TV off. It sounds like a small thing, but it's the difference between talking and half talking.

Weekly or monthly is your call. A weekly check-in tends to be shorter, because there's less to cover and nothing has had long to build up. A monthly one gives you more to look back on, which suits couples whose weeks blur together or who already talk a lot day to day. If you can't decide, try weekly for a month and see whether it feels like too much.

Agree up front that once you've been through the questions, you're done. That's what keeps it light enough to come back to. A weekly check-in can sit comfortably inside twenty or thirty minutes.

If something comes up during the week that you want to bring to it, jot a word or two on your phone so you don't forget. That's all the preparation it needs.

## What questions should you ask in a relationship check-in?

These are check-in questions for couples to ask each other, and you both answer every one. If you check in monthly, read "week" as "month."

1. What was a good moment this week?
2. How did the week actually go for you?
3. What did you notice about us?
4. Did the week look like the one we wanted?
5. What got in the way?
6. What's one thing we want to pay attention to next week?

The good moment comes first on purpose. It sets the tone, and it's the part that gets skipped when life is busy. It can be tiny, like a walk you took together or the night they cooked so you could rest. Saying it out loud is a small celebration, and it reminds you both that the week wasn't only the hard bits.

"How did the week actually go for you?" is about each person's own days before it's about the relationship. That covers work, sleep, energy, a friend who's struggling, or whatever's been taking up room in your head. The word "actually" matters, because it asks for the honest version instead of "fine, busy." Often the reason a week felt off between you is right here, in stress one of you was carrying that the other only half knew about.

"What did you notice about us?" turns toward the two of you. Keep it to things you saw. "We barely ate together" or "we laughed more than usual" is something you noticed, and it's easy to talk about. "You were distant" is a verdict, and it puts the other person on the defensive. Talking in "we" keeps you on the same side of the table.

"Did the week look like the one we wanted?" needs you to have said what you wanted, so the first time you ask it, it's really a question about what you'd like your weeks to have in them. That might be a couple of evenings without phones, time for one person's side project, or seeing friends together. After that, the question has something to compare against. The honest answer is often "partly," and that's normal.

"What got in the way?" looks at the week itself. It might be a late shift, a cold, or a weekend that filled up with other people's plans. Some of those you can change and some you can't, and it helps to know which is which before you plan the next week.

The last question is where the check-in turns forward. Pick one thing and make it small enough that you'd both bet on it happening, like eating at the table twice or keeping Saturday morning free. Next time, question four has something real to look at.

Once you've done this a few times, change the set to suit you. If one question never lands, drop it or put your own in its place.

## How do you keep it from turning into keeping score?

You both answer each question, and you take turns going first. When it's your partner's turn, listen and ask about what they said before you give your side. "What made it feel like that?" will get you further than "well, I was busy too."

Talk about what happened rather than who did it. A check-in goes sour fast when it becomes a tally of who cooked more or who was late more often. You'll both have weeks where you carried more and weeks where you carried less. You're looking at the week so you can decide what to do next, together.

If something stings, say so, and slow down. It's fine to pause, get a glass of water, and come back to it. If a topic turns out to be bigger than one evening, say that too, and give it its own time. The check-in itself should stay something you both look forward to.

Finish on the one thing for next week, and maybe something you're each looking forward to. Ending there leaves you both thinking about what's coming.
