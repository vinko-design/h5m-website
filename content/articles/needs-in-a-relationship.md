Needs in a relationship are the things each of you relies on to feel safe and cared for with the other person. They include trust, being heard, time together, and help when life is busy. To talk about them, you each name a few specific needs in your own words. Then you take turns saying them as something you'd like, and together you pick one next step to try and look back on later.

Your needs won't always match your partner's, and that doesn't have to mean trouble. Sometimes one of you needs encouragement with a new job, or help with the mornings, and the other is glad to do it once they know. Saying a need out loud gives your partner a real chance to respond, so neither of you has to guess.

## What counts as a need in a relationship?

A need is something that, when it's missing for a while, leaves you feeling uneasy or worn down in the relationship. When it's there, you probably don't think about it much. Some needs are about the two of you, like trust or affection. Others are about your own life, where your partner's support makes a difference, like a course you're studying for or sleeping better.

Basic needs in a relationship can sound abstract when you list them, so treat the ones below as prompts. They're a place to start thinking, not a complete list, and yours may be worded quite differently:

- Feeling safe enough to be honest
- Trusting that you'll each do what you say
- Being listened to when something's on your mind
- Affection, in whatever form feels right to you
- Time together, and some time on your own
- Practical help, or encouragement with something you're working toward

A need gets much easier to talk about once it's specific. "Being listened to" might turn into "ten minutes when I get home where we put our phones down and ask about each other's day." "Encouragement" might turn into "asking how the job search is going, without me having to bring it up." The specific version shows your partner what the need looks like on an ordinary Tuesday, and that's something they can actually do.

## What if you go blank when someone asks what you need?

If you go blank, there's a gentler way in. "What do I need?" is a big question, and it can make you reach for what you think you *should* need. Smaller questions about your real days tend to work better.

Try thinking back over the last few weeks and asking yourself a few of these. When did I feel most relaxed with my partner, and what was happening? When did I feel a bit low or alone, even with them in the room? What do I find myself wishing for on the way home? Is there something I keep almost asking for and then dropping?

Your answers don't need to be tidy. "I liked it when we cooked together last Sunday" might be pointing at time together, or at doing something side by side without screens. "I felt a bit invisible at your friend's party" might be pointing at wanting your partner to check in with you when you're somewhere new. Follow the feeling first and find the words for the need afterwards.

If it's easier for you to say what your partner needs than what you need, notice that too. It's a good reason to give yourself some quiet time with these questions before you talk together.

## How can a list of needs and wants help?

Reading a list of needs and wants in a relationship can work like a mirror. You go down the lines and notice which ones make you react. Maybe one makes you think "yes, that's been missing," or another makes you realize something is going well. The list helps you find your own words.

Use it for yourself. It isn't a checklist to hold up against your partner, and it isn't a list of conditions they have to meet. If you catch yourself ticking off what they do or don't do, put the list down and come back to your own experience.

You also don't need to sort every item into "need" or "want" before you talk. For this conversation, what matters is noticing what's important to you right now and being able to say it.

## How do you each name a few needs together?

A short exercise gives you both a fair, even start. You'll need some paper or a note on your phone each, and an unhurried hour when neither of you is about to leave.

1. On your own, write up to three things you need right now.
2. Make at least one about your own life and one about the two of you.
3. Next to each, write what it would look like on an ordinary day.
4. Take turns reading one need aloud while the other listens.
5. The listener says back what they heard and asks one question.
6. Look at both lists together and choose one need to start with.

Writing alone first means each of you puts down what's true for you, before hearing the other person's answers. Three is a ceiling. If only one thing comes to mind, that's plenty.

When you read your needs aloud, swap turns after each one, so neither of you reads a whole list while the other waits. The listener's job is to understand. Saying back what you heard, something like "So you'd like us to have dinner at the table a couple of nights a week, without the TV on?", lets your partner hear whether you've got it right. One question after that, such as "What would make those dinners feel good for you?", is usually enough.

Then lay both lists side by side. You may find the same need on both, worded differently, which makes a natural place to start. One person's need might help the other's too. If you've written "more evenings out" and your partner has written "feeling like we still have fun," those fit together nicely. Some needs may ask for the same evening or the same money. That's worth knowing, and it doesn't have to be settled tonight.

To choose the one to start with, look for a need that matters to at least one of you and that you could do something about in the next week or two. It can be yours, your partner's, or a shared one. Picking one is a way to begin, and the others stay on the list for later.

## How do you say a need without making it about your partner?

Start from your own experience and what you'd like, then leave space for your partner to answer. "I've been feeling a bit on my own in the evenings, and I'd love it if we could sit together after dinner more often" tells your partner what's going on for you and what would help. "You're always on your laptop" makes them defend themselves before they've even heard what you need.

The same need can be said either way, and the first version is much easier to hear. It leaves room for your partner to explain. They might not have noticed, or they might be dealing with something you don't know about yet.

Here's how that might sound in real life.

"I've realized I need a bit more help in the mornings. I'm out the door at seven, and I'm starting every day already tired."

"I didn't know it was that rough. What would help most?"

"If you could make the lunches on weekdays, that would make a big difference."

"I can do that. Can I ask for something too? I'd love one evening a week where we don't talk about the house at all."

The asking goes both ways. Once you've said what you need, ask your partner something like "Is there anything you've been needing from me lately?" and give them time to think. They may have an answer ready, or they may need a day to find one. Both are fine. If your partner's answer surprises you, try asking what it would look like day to day before you respond. That helps you hear the need clearly before you react to it.

When it's your turn to listen, a need can sound like criticism even when it isn't meant that way. If you notice yourself getting defensive, try repeating the need back in your own words first. It gives you a moment, and it shows your partner that you're taking it seriously.

## What can you do when a need isn't being met?

Bring it up as something the two of you can look at together. Pick a quiet moment, then ask whether now is a good time or whether later would suit them better, so your partner has a say in when you talk.

When you do talk, describe what's been happening, what you need, and then ask what's going on for them. "We've barely had an evening to ourselves this month, and I really miss it. How has it been for you?" leaves room for your partner's side. You might hear that they've been missing the same thing, or that work has been heavier than you realized. Their needs belong in that conversation as well, and something they need might be part of why yours hasn't been met.

From there, look for something you could both try. If the need turns out to be about time, you'll find practical ideas for [making time for your partner](/blog/how-to-make-time-for-your-partner) in a full week. If what you each need asks for the same time, money, or energy, the guide on [priorities in a relationship when you want different things](/blog/priorities-in-a-relationship) walks through working out a fair trade. And if the need is physical closeness, [bringing back intimacy](/blog/how-to-bring-back-intimacy-in-a-relationship) covers that conversation in more depth.

If you already asked and nothing has changed, you can come back to it and say what you see now. "We talked about the mornings a few weeks ago, and the mornings are still too much. Can we look at it again?" is fair and specific. Talk about the need as it is now, and leave a list of every missed day out of it.

## How do you choose one next step and come back to it?

Pick something small and specific that you're both happy to try. A step you can both check names who does what, and when. "You make the lunches Monday to Thursday" or "we eat at the table on Tuesdays and Fridays" is clear enough. "Be more supportive" is too vague for either of you to act on.

Check that it fits the week you really have. If the week is already full, keep one evening you can both manage. You can add another once that first one is working.

A need is easier to act on when you treat it as one shared step, then look back. In a [year-long study of 148 Hungarian couples who were married or living together](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41042-023-00089-3), more coordination of personal projects, through talking, cooperating, and supporting each other emotionally, went with being further along on those projects a year later, and with higher life satisfaction for both partners. The study is about personal projects, not a list of needs. It's one group of couples, and the study can't fully prove that one thing caused the other. It's still a reason to choose one shared step and later check whether it helped.

Agree on when you'll look back. That could be in a week, in a couple of weeks, or whenever suits the two of you. When the time comes, ask each other whether the step happened and whether it helped. If you'd like a regular conversation that covers more than one need, these [relationship check-in questions](/blog/relationship-check-in) give it a simple shape.

A week can go off course. One of you might be ill, or work might take the evenings. Tell each other what changed, pick a smaller step, and try that. What you need can change too, after a new job, a move, or a busy stretch, so it's worth coming back to your lists every so often and asking what's true now.

When a step works, say so. "I've loved having the mornings easier this week" tells your partner their effort mattered. That's a high-five moment. Mark it in a way that fits the two of you, with a walk after dinner or a simple thank you.
